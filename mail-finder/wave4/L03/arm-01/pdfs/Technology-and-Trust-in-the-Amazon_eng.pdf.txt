Introduction
Voluntary carbon markets are emerging as an increasingly important component of global efforts
to mobilize finance for climate mitigation and nature protection. The VCMI (Voluntary Carbon
Markets Integrity Initiative), through the Core Carbon Principles (CCPs), defines the fundamental
pillars for the origination of high-integrity credits, among which we can highlight Additionality,
Permanence, Robust Quantification, High Governance Standards, Robust Independent ThirdParty Validation and Verification, Transparency, Tracking, and Benefit and Safeguard for
Sustainable Development. As attention to nature-based solutions grows (the 2025 report State of
the Voluntary Carbon Market indicates that nature-based solutions represent 45% of total
transaction volume in 20241), these markets are attracting renewed interest as an alternative to
channel investment toward high integrity forest restoration and conservation. 



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

Nowhere is this discussion more consequential than in the Amazon. As one of the world’s most
critical ecosystems for climate stability and biodiversity, the region sits at the center of global
debates on how nature-based solutions projects and carbon markets can support forest
protection while respecting complex socio-ecological realities and overcoming financial barriers.
In this context, emissions-reduction projects operate within landscapes shaped by diverse
governance systems, land-tenure arrangements, and community dynamics, raising important
considerations related to participation, benefit-sharing, and accountability. These governance
dimensions are examined in greater detail in our Best Practices Guide for Emissions Reduction
Projects in the Brazilian Amazon.



05

Many of the challenges and technical questions currently shaping debates around carbon
markets are not entirely new. Since the early development of these instruments, scholars and
practitioners have examined methodological questions such as baseline construction,
additionality assessment, carbon stock measurement, and the ability of projects to deliver
verifiable climate outcomes. What has evolved in recent years is the scale and sophistication of
these discussions, supported by improved monitoring capabilities, greater data availability, and
rising expectations around transparency and environmental integrity.

This evolving analytical landscape has prompted a new wave of empirical assessments and
technical evaluations that are helping refine existing frameworks and identify opportunities to
strengthen market architecture. Increasingly, attention has turned toward the systems that
underpin market credibility, specially monitoring and verification mechanisms, data
infrastructures, transparency tools, and financial integration platforms. At the same time,
technological innovation has brought advances in digital monitoring, geospatial data systems,
blockchain-based traceability, and financial infrastructure are beginning to reshape how
environmental outcomes are measured, verified, and communicated. 


Within this context, technological innovation is emerging as a central enabler of more robust and
scalable carbon markets. Against this backdrop, this report explores the growing intersection
between carbon markets and technological innovation, with a particular focus on the Amazonian
context. By mapping emerging technologies, highlighting innovative initiatives, and examining
evolving data and market infrastructures, it seeks to illuminate how technological solutions can
strengthen integrity, improve transparency, and support the responsible scaling of carbon
markets in forest regions.

In Brazil, this role extends beyond carbon markets narrowly defined. Nature Techs can already be
understood as one of the strategic segments of the country’s Nature-based Solutions (NbS)
market, while also functioning as enabling infrastructure for other segments such as restoration,
sociobioeconomy, regenerative agriculture, and bioeconomy value chains. In this sense, their
relevance lies not only in improving environmental integrity, but also in making nature-linked
assets more legible, comparable, and investable.

Technology and Trust in the Amazon

The generation of emissions-reduction credits presupposes, as a structural condition, the
existence of land over which property or use rights are clearly defined, legally valid, and duly
attributed to the project proponents. Because mitigation activities are implemented within a
specific territory, only formal landowners, legitimate possessors, or holders of valid concessions
can, in principle, develop emissions-reduction projects on a given area2.



07

In the Amazon, however, this foundational assumption encounters significant structural
constraints. The region has long been characterized by limited state presence, low levels of land
titling, widespread tenure informality, and territorial instability. This context fuels land conflicts,
overlapping claims, and uncertainty regarding rights of possession and use, all of which create
substantial barriers to the certification of carbon projects. As a result, the risks of future legal
challenges, credit invalidation, rising audit costs, reduced investment appetite, and heightened
reputational exposure for developers, certifiers, and buyers are markedly increased.


Although land-tenure categories vary, recent guidance has increasingly recognized the need for
rigorous prior due diligence that combines legal-technical expertise with localized territorial
knowledge. Such diligence must enable the assessment of the legitimacy of ownership or use
rights, since land-tenure uncertainty directly undermines both the environmental and legal
integrity of mitigation outcomes.

Yet in complex land-tenure contexts like the Amazon, legal and territorial auditing is particularly
costly, time-consuming, and technically demanding. Many municipalities encompass vast and
remote areas with severe logistical constraints, limiting access to reliable and up-to-date
information on land regimes. Verifying tenure legitimacy often requires historical reconstruction
of land occupation, consultation with multiple land and environmental authorities, and direct
engagement with multiple stakeholders, activities outside the primary scope of work for
companies functioning as Validation and Verification Bodies (VVBs) in the context of origination
projects.


hese challenges are compounded by the limited integration and automation of land-tenure
systems in Brazil, especially in the Amazon. Despite recent advances driven by decisions of the
National Council of Justice aimed at digitizing and improving the interoperability of land
registries, many notarial offices still rely on physical archives or incomplete digital records3.
Moreover, inconsistencies between notarial records, environmental registries, territorial
monitoring systems, and the methodological criteria adopted by different carbon standards
undermine analytical coherence and heighten project complexity and legal risk. A cascade
effect is then observed. The different certifiers adopt different methods for quantifying CO2
removals, and the different developers adopt different databases for calculating variables such
as land use. Public databases, commonly used, are credible and based on scientific publications;
however, it is often not possible to understand the calculation performed and the extrapolations
made, which contributes to the dispersion of results across the different registered carbon
projects. The absence of integrated and reliable systems thus represents not merely an
operational hurdle, but a structural constraint on the scalability and integrity of emissionsreduction projects in the Amazon.



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

In light of these structural limitations, the application of technologies to land-tenure and
territorial analysis emerges as a strategic tool to mitigate risk, enhance due-diligence efficiency,
and strengthen project integrity. Remote sensing, geospatial analysis, and territorial intelligence
tools, such as the ones developed by Uirapuru, Terranalytics, TerrasApp and LandPrint Earth,
enable the integration of land-use and land-cover data, deforestation histories, territorial
overlaps, and occupation dynamics. This allows for the early identification of tenure
inconsistencies and high-risk areas, even before on-the-ground fieldwork begins.

When combined with cadastral and environmental databases, these technologies significantly

processes. In addition, digital platforms for document management, data interoperability, and
automated auditing help consolidate information on chains of title, administrative

streamlines validation and verification processes for certifiers, auditors, and investors. Within

Here it is also important to recognize how the nature of tenure-related challenges varies across
project types, and how the same set of technologies is required to interrogate different risk layers.
Across REDD+, ARR, and IFM projects, the core technological toolkit remains largely consistent:
remote sensing, cadastral cross-referencing, and geospatial intelligence. What differs is not the
level of technological development, but the type of tenure risk that these tools are expected to
surface and resolve.

In Conservation (REDD+) projects, tenure challenges tend to be most visible and structurally
acute. These projects often cover large and remote areas where overlapping claims are common.
The central question is whether the project proponent holds a formally valid and uncontested
claim to the area, and whether this claim is sufficiently durable to support long-term conservation
outcomes. In this context, territorial technologies play a critical role in identifying overlaps,
inconsistencies, and areas of heightened legal risk at scale. 

Platforms such as Uirapuru and Terranalytics are particularly well suited to the identification of
structural tenure risks, especially in contexts such as REDD+ projects, where overlapping claims,
fragmented land governance, and limited documentation are prevalent. By integrating cadastral
records, environmental registries, and geospatial data, these tools enable the systematization of
complex territorial information and support the early detection of inconsistencies related to
ownership, possession, and land-use rights. Their primary contribution lies in reconstructing and
organizing dispersed land-tenure data to inform legal due diligence and risk screening processes
at scale.



09

In Restoration (ARR) projects, tenure conditions may appear more straightforward, particularly in
areas classified as degraded land, such as former pasture or abandoned agricultural areas.
However, the tenure inquiry shifts from control over existing forest cover to the legitimacy of landuse transformation. The key question becomes whether the proponent has the legal and
environmental right to convert land use and claim carbon benefits from that transition. In the
Amazon, this often requires demonstrating compliance with legal reserve and permanent
preservation area requirements under the Forest Code, as well as reconstructing land-use history
to substantiate degradation claims. 


Terras App, plays a critical role in contexts where tenure analysis depends on cross-verification
between legal, environmental, and land-use dimensions, which is particularly relevant for ARR
projects. By integrating multiple datasets, including satellite imagery, land registries, and
environmental compliance data, into a unified analytical environment, the platform allows for the
validation of land-use history, verification of legal reserve and permanent preservation area
requirements, and assessment of whether land-use transformations are consistent with regulatory
frameworks. Its strength lies in enabling a dynamic and layered analysis of territorial compliance
over time.

In Improved Forest Management (IFM) projects, tenure is often assumed to be more
straightforward due to the presence of formal documentation, such as concessions,
management plans, or private ownership titles. However, this apparent clarity can obscure a
different and equally critical layer of risk. While documentation may be formally valid, it does not
necessarily reflect who effectively occupies, manages, or holds customary rights over the land.
Cases in the Amazon have demonstrated situations where projects were developed in public or
collectively used areas based on formal or publicly available land data, without the knowledge or
consent of the traditional communities responsible for managing those territories.


In this sense, the challenge in IFM is not the absence of data or less developed technological
tools, but the need to interrogate whether formal tenure reflects legitimate, uncontested, and
socially recognized rights, including customary and collective forms of land use. The role of
technology, therefore, is not only to validate documentation, but to reveal potential
discrepancies between formal records and territorial reality.


LandPrint Earth is particularly relevant in addressing traceability and transparency gaps across
all project types, especially in cases where formal documentation exists but does not fully
capture the complexity of territorial use and control, as often observed in IFM contexts. By
connecting land-tenure, environmental, and production-related data into an integrated system,
the platform enhances the traceability of environmental assets and supports the verification of
their territorial origin. This is especially important for identifying discrepancies between formally
registered rights and actual patterns of land occupation and use, including those involving
customary or collective governance systems.



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

This distinction is fundamental. For buyers, auditors, and certifiers, the assessment can no longer
be limited to verifying whether a title is formally valid; it must extend to evaluating whether that
title reflects legitimate, uncontested, and socially recognized rights of use and control over the
territory. Across project types, technology plays a central role in enabling this shift. Its function is
mobilized to interrogate different layers of territorial risk, depending on the context. Rather than
mapping onto specific project categories, these tools operate as a complementary analytical
stack, collectively enabling a more granular, transparent, and context-sensitive understanding of
land tenure, and, ultimately, a more robust assessment of project integrity in the Amazon.

Building Governance
and Bridging the
Evidence Gap
One of the central challenges observed in emission reduction projects, that has driven significant
criticism of the voluntary carbon market in recent years, relates to weaknesses in data and
evidence infrastructure, which directly undermine project governance and credibility. Under the
current model, validation relies predominantly on static forms of evidence, such as technical
reports, periodic audits, satellite imagery, and documentary records, which capture only
temporal snapshots of project implementation.



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

This approach reveals a fundamental structural limitation. While emission reduction activities
and governance processes unfold continuously and dynamically within the territory, their
verification remains fragmented over time, difficult to access, and heavily mediated by
intermediaries. As a result, the production, organization, and communication of evidence depend
on lengthy audit cycles, limiting the ability to conduct independent, real-time assessments of
what is actually being implemented. At the same time, core governance elements, including
community participation, decision-making processes, and benefit-sharing, are often recorded in
a declaratory manner, without mechanisms to track their execution over time.



12

These limitations generate significant technical and operational consequences. First, they create
information asymmetries between project developers, standard-setters, and buyers: while
developers retain privileged access to operational data, other actors rely on filtered, periodic
information that is difficult to independently verify. Second, the absence of continuous evidence
increases the risk of misalignment between what is reported and what occurs on the ground,
making it harder to identify implementation gaps, operational deviations, or governance failures.
Additionally, reliance on long audit cycles increases transaction costs and reduces system
efficiency, making it less responsive to the dynamic nature of territories.


In this context, the core fragility lies not only in the methodological design of projects, but in the
inability to demonstrate, in a continuous and verifiable manner, that reported activities are
effectively being implemented, and that declared governance structures operate in practice. In
other words, this reflects a systemic failure to translate dynamic, real-world processes into
accessible, traceable, and verifiable evidence over time. This gap affects both private projects
and community-based initiatives, albeit in different ways.

Building Governance and
Bridging the Evidence Gap

In private emission reduction projects, this gap primarily manifests as a challenge in
demonstrating the effective execution of planned activities. Despite the existence of robust
methodologies and formal validation frameworks, verification remains dependent on discrete
evidence points, resulting in limited visibility between audit cycles. In practice, activities such as
restoration, forest management, or territorial protection may be described and reported, but not
continuously tracked in real time, creating a disconnect between planning, implementation, and
verification. This disconnect is seen across project types, but closing the gap requires disparate
technical solutions. This gap introduces a form of non-verifiable implementation risk,
undermining confidence in the integrity of the credits generated.


In community-based projects, this structural gap becomes even more critical, as it affects the
ability to demonstrate legitimate governance, effective participation and co-benefit generation.
The State of the Voluntary Market 20254 emphasizes that the voluntary market buyers tend to
prefer credits generated from projects that provide environmental and social co-benefits.
Elements such as Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC), community participation, benefitsharing, and decision-making processes are often included in project documentation, yet their
verification remains largely limited to formal records. As a result, it becomes difficult to
continuously and transparently assess who participated, how decisions were made, and whether
benefits are being distributed as agreed. This creates risks of purely declaratory participation,
weakens the verification of territorial legitimacy, and increases the likelihood of conflicts and
external scrutiny.



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

In light of these challenges, simplifying processes, enhancing transparency, and expanding
access to data emerge as key pillars for strengthening legitimacy and building integrity
ecosystems in carbon markets. By reducing operational complexity and making information more
accessible, these shifts bring project implementation closer to the capacity for verification by
buyers, communities, and civil society. In this sense, the development of continuous, accessible,
and verifiable evidence infrastructures represents a critical turning point for restoring trust and
reinforcing the integrity of the voluntary carbon market. These same functions are increasingly
relevant beyond carbon origination itself, as NbS and bioeconomy markets also depend on
traceability, governance, impact verification, and lower transaction costs in order to attract
capital and demand.



13

In response to these structural limitations, a new generation of technological solutions has
emerged to enable continuous evidence generation and direct monitoring of activities on the
ground. However, the nature of the evidence gap varies across project types. In conservation and
reduced deforestation projects, where governance legitimacy is traditionally more challenging,
tools that enable continuous documentation of FPIC processes and benefit-sharing mechanisms
are essential to closing the information gap and rebuilding buyer confidence. In restoration
projects, while governance remains important, the primary governance evidence gap is
operational: technologies that enable continuous, field-level verification of planting,
maintenance, and survival activities address the disconnect between project documentation and
on-the-ground implementation. For improved forest management projects, the evidence
challenge centers on demonstrating that harvesting practices have materially changed from the
baseline regime. In this context, forestry monitoring applications such as Agerpoint, which
leverage handheld, IoT-enabled devices for granular forest carbon measurement, can serve as
verifiable evidence that management interventions are being operationalized as described.

Complementarily, the Carbon Justice Standard advances this agenda by integrating a social
integrity standard with a digital implementation platform. Through structured workflows,
educational modules, and participatory MRV tools, it enables the continuous recording,
monitoring, and verification of governance processes. This includes not only the
operationalization of FPIC requirements, but also the incorporation of broader dimensions related
to ecosystem services, cultural practices, and local governance systems. In doing so, it
transforms governance into a continuous evidence infrastructure capable of demonstrating, over
time, not only formal compliance, but also project legitimacy, territorial embeddedness, and
contributions to multiple ecosystem values.



14

Taken together, these approaches signal a structural shift in carbon markets: from a model based
on periodic assessments and static evidence to one grounded in continuous, territorially
anchored, and socially embedded evidence systems. As these solutions progress and available
data products increase, structural improvements will be made to these tools to reflect changing
methodological requirements such as Verra’s requirement for “precision FPIC” - identifying the
most material contributions not the cheapest5. By enabling data to be generated, organized, and
validated by multiple actors, these solutions make it possible to translate the complexity of
territories into accessible interfaces through which stakeholders can directly and continuously
monitor project implementation.


This ability to follow project execution “in the palm of one’s hand” represents a fundamental shift
in how the market operates. By reducing the distance between implementation and verification,
these systems not only enhance transparency, but also rebuild the trust mechanisms that
underpin demand for carbon credits, positioning continuous evidence as a core asset for
integrity, traceability, and credibility.

Within the assessment of environmental integrity in emissions-reduction projects, a second
structural point of friction emerges at the methodological level: the definition of the baseline
scenario. While land-tenure uncertainty undermines the legitimacy of interventions at the territorial
level, baseline construction directly conditions the measurement of additionality and,
consequently, the credibility of claimed climate outcomes. According to the Integrity Council for
the Voluntary Carbon Markets, the baseline represents the hypothetical situation that would occur
in the absence of mitigation incentives, constructed using data intended to reflect the most likely
land-use trajectories had the project not been implemented6.


The analysis of land-use history is a key variable in ARR, IFM, and REDD+ projects, as it is essential
for defining both eligibility under different standards and methodologies, and the baseline,
additionality, and robust quantification. Empirical research shows that deforestation rates used to
establish baselines are typically derived from historical averages adjusted to regional reference
data and then proportionally applied to project areas, generating deforestation-risk maps. These
inputs are used to model a “without-project” scenario and a “with-project” scenario; the
comparison between the two enables the estimation of avoided deforestation attributable to
project activities, that is, additionality.


Technical innovation is already opening the door for more robust and defensible dynamic baselines.
Geospatial embeddings are one such innovation, enabling large quantities of diverse data to be
compressed, creating an forest “fingerprint” for the parcel. Unlike other geospatial data,
embeddings consider the interaction of the data layers at play rather than simply noting layer’s
changes independently. Organizations like Renoster are using Google’s AlphaEarth embeddings to
more efficiently match control plots with project plots when constructing baselines.

However, because the baseline is a theoretical and mathematical construction, and despite
being grounded in historical deforestation data, it remains a probabilistic estimate rather than a
directly observable measure. During project implementation, multiple exogenous variables, such
as economic shifts, public policy changes, land-use pressures, or local social dynamics, may
influence deforestation patterns without being fully captured by the models employed. This limits
the precision with which emission reductions can be attributed to specific project interventions. 


Thus, the baseline outcomes can be significantly influenced by the algorithms and technical
assumptions used to generate risk maps, which vary across methodologies. As demonstrated in
recent empirical studies, such methodological flexibility can lead to substantial variability in
estimated results, with baselines differing by an order of magnitude across approaches, creating
incentives to select methodologies that artificially maximize credit issuance. These findings have
contributed to a broader debate regarding how methodological design can influence the
credibility and comparability of reported mitigation outcomes.


Even where counterfactual approaches are applied, however, reliance on technical modeling and
theoretical projections alone may still produce uncertainty regarding the volume of credits
ultimately generated. Given this context, to generate more robust and accurate evidence of real
project performance, methodological tools must move beyond exclusive reliance on forest
carbon stock estimates. They should incorporate counterfactual analyses, observable spatial
data, and explicit consideration of the socio-environmental dynamics associated with
deforestation and forest degradation in the area.

In this context, complementing counterfactual instruments and territorial data with nationally or
jurisdictionally defined baselines, periodically updated, and with regionally standardized carbonstock default values can establish shared comparative parameters across certification bodies.
Such harmonization would enhance predictability in credit estimates and reduce methodological
dispersion. More frequent baseline updates, grounded in recent deforestation trends and
informed by structurally comparable synthetic control areas or coupled human–natural systems
models, could further improve accuracy.

Adopting these strategies would allow projects within the same region to apply comparable
parameters for additionality accounting, while curbing disproportionate baseline inflation by
anchoring estimates to jurisdiction-level emissions-reduction data. Beyond these measures,
technological innovation is essential to overcoming the inherent limitations of approaches based
solely on theoretical projections, thereby strengthening environmental integrity and the
credibility of mitigation outcomes.


While the academic and policy debate has highlighted important limitations in baseline
construction, it is equally necessary to acknowledge that methodological innovation within the
carbon-market ecosystem is already taking place in practice. In fact, many existing
methodologies have undergone successive revisions and are applying technological innovation,
precisely to improve certainty, incorporate more refined spatial data, and better reflect the
territorial dynamics that shape land use change and deforestation patterns in tropical forest
regions.


A growing number of methodological frameworks already incorporate several of the
improvements discussed in the literature, including shorter baseline update cycles as well as the
incorporation of performance benchmarks on Agriculture, Forestry, and Other Land Use (AFOLU)
sector methodologies, enhanced integration of remote sensing and geospatial data, and
stronger consideration of jurisdictional and land-use governance dynamics. These developments
indicate that parts of the methodological ecosystem are progressively adapting to the socioecological complexity of regions such as the Amazon, rather than remaining fixed in earlier
analytical models.



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

Against this backdrop, a number of emerging certification systems are experimenting with
approaches that attempt to translate these methodological improvements into operational
practice. In this regard, initiatives such as Tero Carbon and Equitable Earth provide illustrative
examples of how methodological evolution is already being implemented within the voluntary
carbon market.



19

Rather than representing purely conceptual alternatives, these initiatives are developing
certification frameworks and methodological approaches that explicitly seek to align carbon
accounting with the legal, socio-environmental, and productive realities of the Amazon region. By
integrating territorial governance considerations, more conservative accounting parameters,
and more frequent methodological revisions, these frameworks contribute to a more contextsensitive and empirically grounded accounting of climate benefits.


As carbon accounting methodologies evolve to incorporate more dynamic and context-sensitive
approaches, technological tools are becoming central to operationalizing these improvements at
scale. Greater attention to these initiatives may therefore play an important role in informing the
continued evolution of carbon-market methodologies toward more credible, territorially
grounded, and scientifically robust frameworks for climate mitigation in the Amazon region.

Following the uncertainties associated with baseline construction and additionality estimation, 

a third central issue emerges in the debate on the environmental integrity of emissions-reduction
projects: permanence. Permanence refers to the capacity of emissions reductions or removals to
endure over time, ensuring that climate benefits are not reversed in the short or medium term
after payments are made. According to the Science Based Targets initiative, permanence relates
to “the longevity of a carbon reservoir and the stability of its stocks, considering the management
regime and the disturbance environment in which it is embedded”13.



21

In forest-based projects, carbon sequestration and emissions-reduction activities are frequently
exposed to external risks such as prolonged droughts, wildfires, extreme weather events, and
anthropogenic pressures. These factors can undermine forest cover and, consequently, 

the stability of carbon stocks over time. As a result, it is not possible to guarantee with full
certainty that mitigation activities will be sustained throughout the project lifetime, given that
climatic shocks or human actions may threaten the permanence of achieved outcomes14.


Beyond environmental risks, economic and institutional challenges also play a decisive role.
Empirical evidence indicates a recurring pattern in forest projects: once payments or financial
incentives cease, conservation outcomes may be partially or fully reversed. This dynamic
highlights the importance of designing mitigation models capable of maintaining conservation
incentives and governance structures beyond the initial project cycle, thereby supporting more
durable climate outcomes.

Depending on the methodological approach adopted, risks of non-permanence, and the social,
physical, and economic drivers behind them, may or may not be internalized. To address these
risks, most major carbon standards and registries employ buffer pools, whereby a share of issued
credits is withheld as a reserve to compensate for potential future reversals. In this arrangement,
buffer pools function as a risk-sharing mechanism that internalizes negative environmental
externalities by allocating a portion of project-generated credits to cover cancellations or
suspensions, thereby preserving the aggregate climate value of realized emissions reductions.
These mechanisms represent an important institutional innovation within carbon markets,
allowing permanence risks to be collectively managed across project portfolios.


At the same time, the scientific literature15 points to alternatives to the traditional logic of longterm permanence commitments. Research suggests that well-designed shorter permanence
assessment periods can, under certain conditions, support the protection and expansion of
carbon sinks. This approach emphasizes continuous flows of atmospheric carbon removal into
short-term stocks which, through periodic renewal, active management, and recurring incentives,
can deliver effective storage durability comparable to, or even exceeding, that of static longterm commitments. This perspective highlights the role of adaptive management and continuous
stewardship in sustaining climate benefits over time.



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

Given the uncertainties surrounding permanence, particularly in forest projects exposed to
climatic and anthropogenic risks, there has been significant progress in the deployment of
continuous monitoring technologies to reduce information asymmetries and enhance early
detection and response to reversal events. High-frequency remote sensing, near-real-time
geospatial monitoring, and recurrent digital verification now make it possible to track forestcover dynamics throughout the project cycle, strengthening transparency and traceability of
carbon stocks. These technological developments are increasingly enabling permanence to be
monitored as a dynamic process rather than a static assumption.



22

However, these technological developments do not eliminate permanence risks, they improve the
capacity to anticipate, identify, and respond to emerging threats, such as deforestation
pressures, land-use change trends, and illegal activities, thereby reducing the likelihood and
potential magnitude of reversals. When combined with territorial governance and protection
mechanisms, such systems enable a more proactive approach to risk management, in which
permanence is supported through continuous monitoring, early warning signals, and timely
intervention.


Initiatives such as PrevisIA exemplify this trend by applying predictive analytics, based on historic
deforestation, satellite data and IA to anticipate deforestation risks and identify areas under
heightened pressure before reversals occur. By supporting ex ante risk assessment and territorial
intelligence, can strengthen the capacity of projects and jurisdictions to act preventively rather
than react only after forest loss has taken place.

Beyond direct land-use threats, permanence risk also extends to the climatic knock-on effects
that deforestation in non-project areas can have on project sites — and this dynamic has now
been successfully modeled in the Amazon. Using AWS ParallelCluster, an open-source highperformance computing tool, researchers simulated how deforestation disrupts regional rainfall
patterns, demonstrating that forest loss in one area can degrade downstream ecosystems
through broken climatic feedback loops16. As models like these mature, project developers will be
better equipped to incorporate endogenous Amazonian climate risks — such as shifting
precipitation and tipping-point dynamics — into their permanence assessments and long-term
project planning.


These predictive approaches are further strengthened when combined with instant monitoring
and community-based alert systems. Rainforest Labs contributes by enabling the continuous
generation of locally grounded environmental data, improving situational awareness over forest
conditions and territorial dynamics. ForestLink complements this logic through real-time
reporting systems that allow communities to transmit alerts on illegal activities directly from the
field, thereby shortening the time between detection and response. Rainforest Defense, in turn,
focuses on converting monitoring data and alerts into rapid territorial protection and
enforcement actions, addressing one of the most critical dimensions of permanence: the ability
to respond operationally to identified threats.

Taken together, these technological and governance-oriented innovations illustrate a broader
shift in how permanence is being addressed within the carbon-market ecosystem. Rather than
treating permanence as a static methodological assumption, these approaches frame it as a
dynamic risk-management challenge shaped by anticipation, monitoring, territorial defense, and
response capacity. In this sense, they help create the conditions under which permanence
becomes more likely, aligning mitigation efforts with the ecological and governance realities of
forest regions such as the Amazon.

Monitoring, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) encompasses the set of processes and technical
instruments designed to track the implementation of emissions-reduction activities and to
substantiate the effectiveness of the climate outcomes claimed in project documentation. In this
sense, MRV serves as a foundational pillar supporting trust, transparency, and scalability in
voluntary carbon markets, providing the technical foundation through which climate outcomes
can be monitored, verified, and reliably communicated to a wide range of stakeholders.



25

Beyond validating baseline assumptions, MRV plays an essential role in substantiating the
empirical information reported by project developers, by demonstrating outcomes actually
delivered during implementation17. It is thus essential not only for quantifying emissions
reductions, but also for documenting tangible evidence of forest conservation, enabling an
assessment of whether methodological projections hold in practice.

As expectations around data accuracy and accountability continue to evolve, MRV frameworks
are undergoing continuous refinement and technological enhancement. Improvements in data
quality, more frequent data collection, enhanced geospatial information, and broader
accessibility of underlying evidence are progressively strengthening the capacity of these
systems to document climate outcomes, contributing to more continuous verification processes,
reinforcing confidence in reported results, and supporting the responsible expansion of voluntary
carbon markets.


While the core MRV technologies, remote sensing, field-based data collection, continuous
monitoring systems, are applied across REDD+, IFM, and ARR projects, the specific variables
being monitored and the risks being addressed differ meaningfully by project type.
Understanding these differences is essential for deploying technological solutions where they are
most needed and for ensuring that MRV systems are calibrated to the actual risks each project
type presents. Within this landscape, some solutions operate as transversal infrastructure
relevant across all project types, while others are more specifically suited to the monitoring
demands of a particular context18. 


A foundational layer of MRV infrastructure serves all three project types by addressing shared
needs: data integrity, continuous verification, and reduction of information asymmetries. Regen
Network provides blockchain-based ecological monitoring infrastructure that enables the
continuous recording and decentralized verification of field-based environmental data, creating
auditable records accessible to multiple stakeholders throughout the project lifecycle regardless
of project type. Shamba Network complements this by deploying remote sensing and
cryptographic data oracles to ensure the integrity and traceability of climate and ecological
data used in carbon accounting.


Beyond carbon metrics, these strategies also reflect the prices and values of the credits
themselves. There is a growing recognition that land-use credits can command additional
premiums when they demonstrably contribute to biodiversity conservation and ecosystem
services, reinforcing the need for more robust and credible monitoring systems. As highlighted by
Ecosystem Marketplace in the State of the Voluntary Carbon Market 2025S19, value differentiation
is increasingly tied not only to emissions outcomes, but also to measurable co-benefits such as
biodiversity protection and ecosystem integrity. In this context, a growing range of tools is being
deployed transversally, including environmental DNA (eDNA) tracking, community-based
biodiversity monitoring, and remote sensing systems.


At the same time, innovative solutions are expanding the frontier of biodiversity monitoring
across all project types. Organizations such as Rainforest Connection are advancing bioacoustic
monitoring technologies that use AI-enabled sensors to capture and analyze forest soundscapes.
These systems enable continuous, non-invasive monitoring of biodiversity while also tracking
forest activities in real time. Importantly, this approach goes beyond ecological observation by
supporting the detection of illegal activities such as logging and encroachment, thereby
contributing to enforcement efforts and strengthening environmental governance, illustrating a
shift toward multi-layered monitoring systems that combine different data sources and
technologies to better capture ecological complexity.

In REDD+ projects, MRV is primarily oriented toward detecting forest cover loss and verifying that
deforestation has been avoided within and around the project boundary. The key challenge is
spatial and temporal: ensuring that the absence of deforestation is continuously verifiable, not
just at periodic audit points. At the jurisdictional level, Sylvera20 through its Biomass Atlas,
independently assesses jurisdictional REDD+ credits, as evidenced by its work in the State of Acre
and elsewhere in the Amazon, providing high-capacity baseline generation that underpins
project planning, carbon credit ratings, verification processes and most steps in between. Open
Forest Protocol complements this through its open-source, blockchain-based MRV protocol,
combining mobile data collection with a decentralized network of validators to create tamperresistant, publicly accessible evidence of forest conservation outcomes throughout the project
lifecycle.


In IFM projects, MRV must go beyond forest cover to track whether harvesting activities comply
with approved management plans, in terms of volumes, species, and spatial extent. As
documented in cases such as Brazil's Operation Greenwashing21, approved management plans
can serve as cover for illegal extraction, making compliance monitoring as critical as forest cover
monitoring. This requires tools capable of cross-referencing harvesting authorizations from
systems such as SINAFLOR with satellite-derived canopy data, detecting discrepancies between
declared and actual extraction volumes. In this context, monitoring initiatives that document
activities as they are effectively carried out within project territories represent a critical
differentiator in ensuring high-integrity carbon outcomes, such as Rainforest Connection's
acoustic detection of chainsaw activity, that provides an independent and continuous
verification layer that periodic audits or satellite imagery alone cannot replicate. 



Technology and Trust in the Amazon

In ARR projects, MRV shifts toward verifying the establishment and survival of restored
vegetation, tracking biomass accumulation over time, and demonstrating that land-use change
has actually occurred. This requires different data layers, including soil carbon measurement,
species composition monitoring, and land-use history validation that complement but differ from
the forest-cover-centered approaches used in REDD+ and IFM. Re.Seed addresses this directly by
integrating field data, satellite imagery, and digital records to track restoration activities through
a bottom-up approach, where local actors collect real-time observations using mobile devices,
incorporating local knowledge into operational processes and strengthening data reliability
through shorter monitoring cycles particularly well suited to the iterative nature of restoration.



27

Taken together, these technological developments signal a broader shift in how MRV is being
operationalized across all project types, from periodic, document-based approaches toward
continuous, data-driven systems calibrated to the specific risks each project presents. By
enabling higher-frequency monitoring, multi-source data integration, and enhanced
transparency, these solutions expand the evidentiary basis of carbon accounting, reduce
information asymmetries, and advance MRV toward a more auditable and credible model
aligned with contemporary standards of environmental integrity.

If earlier debates highlighted frictions related to territory, time, and methodological constraints,
leakage exposes an additional layer of risk: the challenge of ensuring that project-scale carbon
interventions are aligned with broader spatial, economic, and productive dynamics. Even where
an emissions-reduction project successfully curbs deforestation or emissions within its defined
boundaries, there remains a risk that these same activities generate adverse environmental
impacts beyond the project area by displacing or intensifying anthropogenic pressures in
adjacent territories. This phenomenon, known as leakage, constitutes a negative externality, as it
leads to an unanticipated loss of net emissions-reduction benefits22.



30

Leakage can manifest in multiple ways. One form occurs when emissions-generating activities
are not truly halted, but rather displaced outside the project boundary, affecting connected
areas, undermining ecosystem services, and generating upstream or downstream emissions
along the relevant value chains23. Another form arises when project outcomes indirectly alter
local or regional economic dynamics, potentially influencing land-use decisions that were not
fully anticipated in the baseline scenario, such as shifts in the supply of goods, services, or
development opportunities24.

As highlighted in the scientific literature25, the use of standardized leakage factors across many
methodologies has been the subject of ongoing discussion regarding their ability to capture the
full diversity of territorial dynamics. This challenge is compounded by the fact that leakage
assessment is not incentive-neutral: identifying higher leakage levels directly reduces the volume
of credits issued, potentially discouraging more rigorous or conservative analyses. For this reason,
recent debates increasingly emphasize the importance of improving methodological
transparency and refining leakage estimation approaches in order to strengthen the credibility of
project outcomes.


Leakage dynamics are typically linked to activities that were not anticipated or incorporated into
the initial calculations of baselines, additionality, and permanence, making leakage intrinsically
connected to how these parameters are constructed. This relationship highlights the importance
of integrating broader territorial and economic dynamics into project design. When potential
drivers of displacement or intensified deforestation are identified early in the project design
phase, leakage can be mitigated or even prevented from the outset through territorial planning,
socio-economic engagement, and a more robust definition of project areas of influence26.


Given the inherent challenges of measuring leakage exclusively at the project scale, there is
growing attention to the role of regional monitoring technologies, integrated spatial analysis, and
supply-chain traceability as complementary tools for detecting and mitigating this risk. Unlike
traditional approaches centered on fixed project boundaries and default leakage values, these
technologies enable the observation of broader territorial dynamics, capturing deforestation
displacement, indirect land-use change, and spillover effects associated with project activities.

time. By providing temporal and spatial granularity, these tools allow for the detection of
emerging trends and shifts in land-use dynamics, although they do not, on their own, establish
causal attribution between project interventions and observed changes.

Beyond the technical parameters that define emissions reductions, such as additionality,
leakage, and permanence, the credibility and scalability of the Voluntary Carbon Market (VCM)
also depend on the quality of its information. While additionality, leakage, and permanence
occupy a central place in technical debates on emissions-reduction projects, the ability of
markets to generate trust ultimately relies on robust systems for transparency, data accessibility,
and traceable environmental accounting. Among these, the development of robust systems for
access to information and transparency plays a particularly important role in strengthening
credit traceability and supporting the consolidation of a market capable of meaningfully
contributing to ambitious climate goals and avoiding double counting. In this context,
transparency and qualified access to information assume a strategic role, not only for buyer
confidence, but also for public governance and the social legitimacy of these instruments.



34

One of the VCM’s core challenges relates to the ongoing development of robust, standardized,
and interoperable systems capable of continuously and coherently tracking the full life cycle of
credits, from issuance and transfer to final retirement. Operationally, this need manifests at
multiple levels. Information essential to assessing environmental and social integrity, such as land
use and land cover, additionality, leakage, permanence, socio-environmental safeguards, and
land-tenure status, is not always available in fully integrated, real-time, auditable, or comparable
formats across projects, methodologies, and registries. Moreover, post-issuance tracking can
vary across registries and systems, sometimes limiting clear identification of ownership,
transaction histories, declared uses, and retirement status. Addressing these information gaps
has therefore become a central priority for strengthening transparency and consistency across
carbon-market actors.

Against this backdrop, technological innovation emerges as a key vector for mitigating these
weaknesses, particularly solutions focused on data integration, qualification, and
standardization. Platforms capable of unifying information from multiple registries,
methodologies, and monitoring systems, while providing reliable data on issuance, transfer, and
retirement, become central to reducing information asymmetries and enhancing market
transparency. In this regard, blockchain-based technologies have attracted particular attention.

By operating through distributed, immutable, and verifiable ledgers, blockchain provides a digital
infrastructure for transparent record-keeping that is resilient to unilateral alteration. This
architecture enables independent third-party verification, with each block validated in a
decentralized manner, strengthening the credibility of recorded data. Such systems can be
applied to a range of environmentally relevant functions, including asset traceability,
tokenization of environmental assets, crypto-financial instruments, decentralized autonomous
organizations, and environmental protocols. 


In parallel, the use of blockchain to decentralized MRV infrastructures, such as Open Forest
Protocol and Regen Network, demonstrates how these technological architectures are being
operationalized to enhance transparency and data accessibility at the project level. By
anchoring field-based evidence, georeferenced data, and monitoring records within verifiable
digital systems, these initiatives contribute to strengthening auditability and traceability across
the lifecycle of environmental assets, reinforcing broader transparency objectives by enabling
continuous, distributed, and independently verifiable access to environmental information.

In carbon markets specifically, decentralized ledgers could link each credit to a structured set of
verifiable metadata, covering project origin, source registry, applied methodology, monitoring
data, independent verification outcomes, transaction history, and retirement status. This design
enables continuous audit trails accessible to regulators, certifiers, investors, buyers, and civil
society alike.


As noted by Christiansen27, blockchain can also facilitate efficient exchange of information
tokens without intermediaries, integrating data on land tenure, socio-environmental safeguards,
implementation evidence, georeferenced information, impact assessments, and MRV processes.
The potential is further expanded through the use of non-fungible tokens (NFTs) differentiated by
the carbon content associated with specific trees or forest assets, allowing finer-grained
valuation and a more direct link between physical environmental assets and their digital
representations28.


Moreover, even where not yet fully operationalized, blockchain offers the technical capacity to
permanently lock or burn NFTs, removing them from circulation and helping prevent double
counting, thereby reinforcing environmental integrity. Permanence risks, for example, could be
partially mitigated by integrating blockchain systems with independent, up-to-date data sources
such as satellite imagery and geospatial information that continuously track vegetation cover
and detect land-use change29.

infrastructure are therefore gaining momentum, helping to position carbon credits more clearly

problems; rather, they bring long-standing structural challenges into sharper focus. Issues related

instruments of climate finance. Methodological uncertainties affect credit credibility; gaps in
monitoring and verification reduce confidence in reported outcomes; and limitations in
transparency, traceability, and market infrastructure constrain the broader economic
functionality of carbon credits. Recognizing these challenges is important, but it is equally
essential to acknowledge that a growing ecosystem of technological solutions is actively working
