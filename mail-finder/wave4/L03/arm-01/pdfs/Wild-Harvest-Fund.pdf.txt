The Wild Harvest Fund is a $5 to $10 million investment fund concept,
paired with a philanthropic facility, that offers concessional loans to
cooperatives and small-to-medium enterprises (SMEs) involved in
‘wild harvest’ products in the Amazon rainforest. These are fruits, nuts,
oils and resins that depend on forest canopy to grow and are usually
harvested by hand by Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities
(IPLCs). The Wild Harvest Fund is designed to start in the Brazilian
Amazon, where 250+ associations and cooperatives source wild
harvest products but face barriers to grow at scale. The Wild Harvest
Fund helps to address these barriers by coupling financing capital
and philanthropic funds to derisk investments along the supply chain.
At scale, the Wild Harvest Fund could help build bioeconomy
businesses across IPLCs lands in Brazil, which are important for global
climate stability. IPLC lands in the country contain over 12.87 billion
metric tons of irrecoverable carbon (9% of the global total).

Wild Harvest
Products

Non-timber forest products (NTFPs) that are either harvested from or
compatible with highly conserved native forests. Wild-harvested
products can include fruits, nuts, seeds, resins, leaves, or even animals.
Examples include açaí, aguaje, andiroba, babaçu, Brazil nuts, breu
branco, camu-camu, copaíba, cumaru (tonka beans), cupuaçu,
jaborandi, jatobá, murumuru, pirarucu, tambaqui, and ucuuba.**

Communitybased

Community engagement is essential to the management of Wild Harvest
Products, as it ensures the integration of local knowledge and best practice
forest management techniques. By supporting sustainable livelihoods, WHPs
play a crucial role in the conservation of tropical forests.

Low-impact

Low-impact management of WHPs prioritizes sustainable harvesting without
disrupting forest ecosystems. This approach minimizes disturbance through
planned harvesting, respecting species ecology, and manual collection, while
preserving biodiversity, soil health, and water quality. In the Brazilian Amazon,
research on the impact of WHP development in protected areas (Rosenfeld, T,
2024) reports positive conservation outcomes.

*The Wild Harvest Fund focus excludes from this definition hunting, agroforestry systems and fibers that require some sort of logging to harvest. The WHF is specifically aimed at improving
sustainability practices for forest products that meet the Wild Harvest criteria, however, it is recognized that the permanence of this intervention relies on communities having diversified
sources of income (that might include hunting, agroforestry for instance). In these cases, it is acceptable if those activities are part of the supply chain, as long as the overall
environmental indicators are met.

Case Study: Copaiba value chain in Calha Norte (Pará)
•

Copaiba trees (copaifera) can be up to 40 meters high and 4
meters in diameter. They grow slowly and can live up to 400 years
(Trindade et al, 2018). These trees contain significant carbon.

•

Product: Copaiba oil is a traditional Amazonian medicinal
product with cultural significance for local communities.

•

Process: The resin is directly extracted from the tree trunk by
Indigenous Peoples, Quilombola or Local Communities without
damaging the tree.

•

Barrier: It is common for communities to lack the resources to
purchase the inputs needed to implement good copaiba
management practices, as well as paying the extractors upon
delivery of the production. This makes it difficult for families to
engage and encourages sales to intermediaries who pay upon
delivery and take care of the logistics and inputs – even though
these intermediaries offer lower prices.

Working capital loans to cooperatives and associations would allow
them to purchase the necessary inputs and pay local managers
upon delivery, gaining production volume and better conditions for
implementing good management practices than would be the case
with individual harvesting and selling to intermediaries.
A capital expenditure loan to a local SME could increase production
volume, implement traceability strategies, and facilitate access to
high value-added markets, increasing revenue for community
enterprises (e.g. doTerra or Natura).

CONTEXT

The Solution: Wild Harvest Fund
The Wild Harvest Fund seeks to address the interconnected barriers to
investing in Wild Harvest Products.
Through its philanthropic facility, it aims to enhance local governance by
empowering local implementation partners. Simultaneously, the
investment fund provides fair and adequate credit access to local
associations, cooperatives and SMEs.

10

•

Status: Concept development (seeking feedback and partners)

•

Region: Brazilian Amazon initially with potential to be replicated in
other Pan-Amazon countries

•

Type of Instrument: Investment Fund with a Philanthropic Facility

•

Type of investments: Main instrument will be concessional loans to
local associations, cooperatives and SMEs engaged in developing or
expanding Wild Harvest Products value chains. Loans can be for
working capital (e.g., payment on delivery of WHPs from local
communities), or capital investments (e.g., boats, machinery)

•

Objectives: (a) increase capital availability for on-the-ground
projects; (b) enhance collaboration with corporates for leveraging
offtake agreements; (c) provide enabling conditions for addressing
repayment risks

IMPLEMENTATION

Case study: Living Amazon Mechanism (Natura)
The Living Amazon Mechanism, proposed by Brazilian cosmetics company Natura&Co, is a
blended finance instrument designed to strengthen socio-biodiversity supply chains and
support IPLCs in the Amazon, from which Natura sources its raw materials.

32

•

Objective: financial structure created to address the lack of financial and technical support for
Natura’s suppliers, which include cooperatives and associations of IPLCs

•

Products: these suppliers provide NTFPs, which is a definition very aligned with the WHPs described in
this blueprint

•

Instrument focus: focuses on offering working capital and technical assistance directly to IPLC
cooperatives and associations. As a result, the initial version, launched in 2023, was relatively small,
totaling USD 2.5 million.

•

Differentiation with WHF: The Wild Harvest Fund differs from Natura’s structure in that it focuses on
leveraging offtake agreements from multiple different companies.

IMPLEMENTATION

Next steps
1. Conduct an analysis of existing players offering similar financial instruments to identify potential synergies
that could enhance the development of the Wild Harvest Fund concept while avoiding overlaps and
reducing transaction costs. Join forces with:
(a) An asset manager that has already launched an investment vehicle with similar thesis; and
(b) Technical assistance providers / pipeline developers who will bring the pipeline of associations,
cooperatives and SMEs seeking financing
2. Refine and build core competencies, ensuring a clear distribution of roles and responsibilities between the
asset manager and the philanthropic facility manager, as well as with other actors providing services to
the Wild Harvest Fund such as WHP traceability, donor and investor reporting, technical assistance,
pipeline assessment, network connections, technological support, and research and development,
among others
3. Develop and validate the financial model based on the engagement with key actors, pipeline developers
and other services providers to calibrate commercial, concessional, and philanthropic capital needs
4. Partner with an asset manager and their already constituted or to be constituted investment vehicle
5. Develop social and environmental safeguards for the WHF, ensuring that the investments in cooperatives
and SMEs do not lead to unintended consequences with IPLCs

6. Fundraise for the Philanthropic Facility that will serve as a side car to the investment vehicle

Having a precise estimate of the market size for WHPs in the Brazilian
Amazon is challenging given its informality, remoteness, and lack of
precise definition of what is wild harvested. However, recent
estimates indicate it could be from US$298 million to US$ 5 billion
annually.
Nobre, C.A., 2023 estimates that:
• R$25,6 billion (~US$5 billion) is the annual market size for 13 key
native products from the Brazilian Amazon (including açaí fruit and
palm, cocoa, Brazil nuts, babassu coconut and oil, cupuaçu,
honey, rubber, buriti, annatto, copaíba, and andiroba)
• By 2050, those 13 key native products could have a market of R$
38,5 billion (~US$ 7.5 billion) and produce 947,000 jobs.
Coslovsky, S., 2021 estimates that:
• Globally, the export market for 64 non-timber forest products
(honey, fish, fruits, shrimp, nuts, oils) is US$176.6 billion per year.

0.17% of this market, with US$298 million.
